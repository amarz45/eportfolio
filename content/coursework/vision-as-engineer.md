A short paper written for COOP 2100 on what I think engineering work should be
aimed at.

The argument is that a lot of the technology people use every day persists not
because it's good but because leaving it is expensive – accumulated files,
habits, and training make switching feel costly enough that better alternatives
don't win by being better. The useful engineering problem, then, isn't only
building something good; it's building something good enough that the cost of
switching stops being the deciding factor.

Writing it clarified something I'd only half-articulated before: the reason I
care about impact over cleverness is that impact is the thing that actually
moves people off the old option.

## Where the argument is weak

The obvious objection is that switching costs aren't irrational, and I don't
fully account for that. When an organisation stays on software everyone already
knows how to use, it's making a defensible trade: retraining is a real expense,
and a better tool that nobody can operate yet is worse in the short run. My
argument treats inertia as something to overcome, when some of it is a rational
assessment I'd make too. What I'd add now is that the engineer's job includes
lowering the switching cost itself – import paths, familiar interfaces,
migration tooling – not only making the destination better.

## Competencies demonstrated

**Professionalism** — forming a defensible position on what engineering work is
for, and stating it plainly enough to be argued with. **Impact on Society and
the Environment** — the paper is about which technology people actually end up
using, which is a question about consequences rather than about code.
**Life-Long Learning** — writing it forced me to articulate a belief I had been
acting on without examining.
